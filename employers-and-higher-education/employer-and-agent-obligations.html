Employer and Agent Obligations

A guide to employment, immigration and recruitment agent obligations when hiring candidates from India.

Audience
EU employers
Scope
Employment, immigration and recruitment agents
  • Email

On this page

    This guide explains what you must do, and must not do, when you hire a candidate from India, and when you use a recruitment agent to do it. Rules and procedures vary by EU Member State, permit type and employment arrangement. Use this guide as an orientation tool and always check the rules that apply in the country where you employ.

    How to use this guide

    Hiring from India involves two sets of obligations. The first is your employment obligations, such as the contract, pay and working conditions. The second is your immigration obligations, such as checking the permit and respecting the worker's right to change employer. EU law sets a common minimum for both, while national law, collective agreements and the conditions of the permit add the detail.

    Your employment contract and the worker's immigration permission are related, but they are not the same thing. A workplace dispute does not automatically decide what happens to the worker's residence status, and a residence permit does not remove the worker's employment rights. Check both sets of rules.

    Before you recruit, make sure you can answer:

    1. Are you able to share information about the salary or salary range in the vacancy notice or ahead of the interview?
    2. Is the agent registered in India and listed on the eMigrate portal?
    3. Who applies for the permit, you or the worker, and who pays the fee?
    4. Which labour authority and which social security scheme apply where you are hiring ?

    1. Recruiting and Advertising

    Share information about the salary or salary range r in the job advertisement or ahead of the job interview. .1 Do not ask what the candidate earns now or earned in a previous job, and do not stop employees from discussing their own pay.1

    If you pay the permit application fee, you cannot recover it from the worker.2

    2. The Employment Contract

    Give the worker the essential terms of the job in writing, from the first working day and no later than the seventh calendar day. These include pay, working time, leave and the notice period.3 For a worker sent to another country, the written terms must also state the currency of pay and any repatriation terms.3

    Other contract rules:

    1. Probation cannot exceed six months, except in exceptional cases such as where it is in the worker's interest.4
    2. You cannot ban the worker from taking additional work outside working hours, apart from limited justified restrictions.4
    3. Training you are required to provide is free for the worker and counts as working time.4
    4. After six months, a worker can ask for more predictable and secure working conditions, and you must reply in writing with reasons.3
    5. International fair recruitment standards say the contract should be in a language the worker understands, given well before the worker leaves India, and protected against substitution.5

    3. The Permit and the Right to Work

    1. Before day one, see the worker's valid permit or other authorisation to work, and keep a copy for as long as the worker is employed.6
    2. Each country decides whether you, the worker, or either of you submits the application. The authority must decide on a complete single permit application within 90 days, with 30 more days for complex cases.7
    3. A refusal must be in writing with reasons, and it can be challenged in court in that country.7
    4. An EU Blue Card requires a work contract or binding offer of at least six months.8
    5. If a worker has worked for you without valid authorisation, you are liable for unpaid wages, taxes and social security contributions, and the worker can complain to recover them.6

    4. Fair Working Conditions

    Give the worker equal treatment with nationals on pay, dismissal, working hours, leave and holidays, equal treatment of men and women, and health and safety. The worker also has the right to join a trade union and to strike.7 Countries must inspect and sanction employers who break these rules.9

    EU working time minimums for every worker:

    1. An average of no more than 48 hours a week including overtime, calculated over up to four months.10
    2. At least 11 consecutive hours of daily rest, a break if the day is longer than six hours, and 24 hours of weekly rest.
    3. At least four weeks of paid annual leave, which money cannot replace except when employment ends.

    Your contract can give more than these minimums, but not less.

    Social security and tax

    Equal treatment also covers certain branches of social security and tax benefits.9 Among EU countries, India has social security agreements with Austria, Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal and Sweden.11 If an Indian employer posts a worker to you in one of these countries, the worker can hold a Certificate of Coverage issued by India’s Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO),the Indian social security body. This certificate shows the worker stays in the Indian scheme and does not have to pay into the host country’s scheme If you hire the worker directly, confirm in writing which scheme covers the worker before the contract is signed.

    5. Changing Employer or Ending Employment

    Some permits are linked to an employer or to set conditions, and others allow more flexibility. Check what the permit requires before you hire a worker who is already in the country, and before you end an employment.

    1. A single permit holder can change employer. A country may require notification, may apply a labour market check, and may set a minimum period with the first employer of up to six months.7,2,12
    2. Where notification is required, the change can be suspended for up to 45 days. In the Netherlands, for example, the worker can start with the new employer if there is no objection in that time.2,13
    3. If you end the employment, the permit does not end by itself. The worker has at least three months to find new work, or six months after two years with the permit, and three more months if the unemployment follows abusive working conditions.7,14
    4. Blue Card holders: in the first 12 months a country may require notification and a labour market check. After 12 months it can require notification only.8 In Czechia, for example, notification goes to the Ministry of the Interior within three working days.15

    6. Working With Recruitment Agents in India

    Under Section 10 of the Emigration Act, 1983, no person or agency can act as a recruiting agent in India without a Registration Certificate from the Protector General of Emigrants.16 An agent recruits for you under a power of attorney from you.17

    A registered agent must:

    1. Display the registration number on the office signboard and in every advertisement, including on social media.18,19
    2. File copies of advertisements and employment contracts with the Protector of Emigrants, who authenticates the contracts.18
    3. Use a contract signed by you, the agent and the candidate.19
    4. Issue a receipt for every amount collected.16
    5. Appear on the List of Active Recruiting Agents at emigrate.gov.in, which you can check yourself.19

    An agent must not:

    1. Charge more than Rs 30,000 plus 18% GST (about €273 plus GST) in service charges. Charging more is an offence under the Act.19,17
    2. Charge repatriation costs.18
    3. Promise a visa. No agent can guarantee one.20
    4. Work only through messaging apps. The Ministry of External Affairs reports overcharging of Rs 0.2 to 0.5 million(about €1,800 to €4,500) by unregistered agents on WhatsApp and similar channels, and says this amounts to human trafficking.19

    When a complaint is made against a registered agent, the Protector General of Emigrants issues a show cause notice under Section 14 of the Act and directs the agent to resolve it.16 An agent found to be fraudulent is a legal and reputational risk to you. Agree in writing that the agent will collect nothing from candidates beyond what Indian law allows and ask to see the receipts.

    7. Who Pays for Recruitment

    The ILO standard is that workers should not be charged recruitment fees or related costs, and it applies to employers and the recruiters they use.5 Countries apply it differently.

    1. India: the agent's service charge is capped at Rs 30,000plus 18% GST (about €273 plus GST), with a receipt.19
    2. Germany: a private agency can charge up to EUR 2,000 for simple placement under the Social Code. Make it in Germany advises that skilled workers from abroad should not have to pay it, and notes there is no licensing scheme for private agencies, so the Indian registration check matters.20
    3. Other EU countries: check specific country where it states a local rule. Otherwise treat the ILO standard as the benchmark

    8. What Employers Must Not Do

    1. Employ a worker before the permit allows it.6
    2. Ask about salary history or stop staff discussing pay.1
    3. Pass recruitment costs to the worker.5
    4. Change the contract after it is agreed.5
    5. Run a probation beyond six months.4
    6. Hold passports, withhold wages or threaten deportation. The ILO treats these as indicators of forced labour.21

    9. Where the Rules Are Still Changing

    Countries had to apply the pay transparency rules by 7 June 2026, and on 10 June only Italy, Slovakia, Lithuania and Malta had complete laws in force.1,22 The Single Permit rules were due on 21 May 2026, and on 15 July 2026 the Commission sent formal notices to 17 Member States for incomplete transposition: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Finland, France, Germany, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia and Sweden.7,23 Ireland and Denmark do not apply the Single Permit or Blue Card Directives.24 In these cases the national procedure may still be changing, so check for specific country and the national authority before you act.

    10. If Something Goes Wrong

    If a problem arises in your recruitment or employment, you do not have to deal with it alone. The correct route depends on the problem and the country.

    1. Stop any payment involving candidates, and keep every contract, advertisement, message, payslip and receipt.
    2. Report an unregistered or overcharging agent to the Protector of Emigrants or through the eMigrate portal.16,17
    3. Cooperate with the national labour inspectorate or other competent authority in your country.
    4. If a worker has worked for you without valid authorisation, pay the back wages, taxes and social security contributions owed.6

    Employer Checklist

    1. Does your advertisement give the pay or pay range before the interview?
    2. Is the agent on the eMigrate list, with a registration number displayed?
    3. Do you have written authorisation for the agent?
    4. Will the contract be signed by you, the agent and the candidate, in a language the candidate understands, before the candidate leaves India?
    5. Does the contract state pay, hours, leave, notice period, probation and repatriation terms?
    6. Have you confirmed that no fee beyond the Indian cap will be collected from the candidate?
    7. Do you know who applies for the permit and who pays the fee?
    8. Will you check the permit before day one and keep a copy?
    9. Do you know which social security scheme covers the worker?
