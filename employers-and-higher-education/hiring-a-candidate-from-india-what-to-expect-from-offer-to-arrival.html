    Overview

    Hiring a candidate from India takes eight steps from offer to arrival, and roughly two to six months as an indicative guide. Timelines vary by Member State and permit type, so employers should consult the relevant national authorities (including their respective embassies in India) for the latest requirements and processing times.

    Ongoing

    Before you start, check three things with your national authority. First, which permit fits the role: the EU Blue Card applies in all Member States except Denmark and Ireland, and other Member States run national permits. Second, the minimum salary for that permit, where one is set. The EU Blue Card has a salary threshold, and some national routes do too, while others only require pay in line with comparable national workers. Third, whether you must register as an employer or sponsor, or use a faster procedure for skilled workers.

    Step 1: Make a compliant job offer

    The offer is the document every later step depends on, because most permits are granted only against a concrete job offer or signed contract.

    What you do

    • State the job title, duties, salary, working hours, start date and contract length.
    • Check that the salary meets the minimum for the permit, where specified, and that the role needs the candidate's qualifications.
    • Make the offer conditional on the permit and visa being granted.
    • Where your country requires it, register as an employer or sponsor first, or show that no suitable local candidate is available (a labour market test). Roles on a national shortage list are often exempt.

    Watch out for: salary thresholds, sponsor rules and labour market tests differ by Member State and permit. Confirm them with your national authority before you make the offer.

    What is a labour market test? Before granting a permit, some Member States check that the job cannot be filled by someone already in their labour market: citizens, EU citizens and lawful residents. The check usually takes one of two forms. The employer must advertise the vacancy for a set period, for example through the public employment service or EURES, or the authority reviews the labour market for that occupation. If a suitable local candidate is found, the permit can be refused.

    Exceptions. The test is often waived for ICT hires:

    • Shortage occupations. Roles on a national shortage list are usually exempt, and ICT roles appear on several lists (see country profiles for more information).
    • High-skilled routes. The EU Blue Card and its national equivalents mostly rely on a qualification and a salary threshold instead of a test. Under the revised EU Blue Card Directive, a Member State may apply a test only where its labour market is seriously disturbed, such as high unemployment in an occupation or region. It must then justify the measure and renew it every six months. A test may also be applied if the holder changes employer in the first 12 months.
    • Employer-specific exemptions. In Ireland, for example, client companies of Enterprise Ireland or IDA Ireland are exempt for the General Employment Permit.

    Step 2: Confirm the candidate's qualifications

    Most skilled-worker permits rely on a higher-education degree. Under the EU Blue Card rules, relevant professional ICT experience can count in place of a degree in some cases.

    What you do

    • Ask for degree certificates, employment references and a CV.
    • Check whether your country needs a formal recognition or comparability statement from its national ENIC-NARIC centre. Regulated professions always do; many ICT roles do not.
    • Keep copies of everything you receive, as the permit application will ask for them.

    Watch out for: recognition can take several weeks, and the length of time varies from one Member States to another, so start it as soon as the candidate accepts the offer.

    Step 3: Apply for the work and residence permit

    The permit lets the candidate live and work in your country. In some Member States the employer applies, in others the candidate does, and in some both are involved. Processing takes from several weeks to a few months, and some countries offer faster procedures for skilled workers.

    What you do

    • Prepare the signed contract or offer, your company registration documents and any sponsor or employer approval.
    • Collect the candidate's passport copy, CV, qualifications and recognition statement.
    • Apply only through the official portal or authority of your country, and keep the approval notice, which the candidate needs for the visa.

    Watch out for: the EU Blue Card covers most Member States, but national permits differ. If the candidate is a manager, specialist or trainee moving from a group company in India, the intra-corporate transfer permit may fit better.

    Step 4: The candidate applies for the entry visa

    Once the permit is approved, the candidate applies at your country's embassy or consulate in India for a long-stay entry visa, often through a visa application centre. This usually involves an appointment, biometrics and supporting documents.

    What you do

    • Send the candidate the approval notice, the contract and a short letter confirming the job.
    • Answer the candidate's questions, and make clear who pays the visa fee.
    • Warn the candidate against paying agents for visa slots or approvals.

    Watch out for: appointment availability varies by city and season, so ask the candidate to book early.

    Type of visa. For a stay of more than 90 days, most Member States require a long-stay national visa, often called a type D visa, issued by the embassy or consulate once the permit is approved. A short-stay Schengen visa (type C) cannot be used to take up work. Some countries combine the steps: in the Netherlands, the employer’s application can include a provisional residence permit (MVV) that serves as entry authorisation. France endorses the long-stay visa “salarié” or “travailleur temporaire” depending on contract length. Germany issues a national visa for qualified employment, and the candidate then applies for the EU Blue Card at the local foreigners authority after arrival. The visa lets the candidate enter, and the residence permit card is usually issued or collected afterwards.

    Step 5: Prepare the move

    This runs alongside the permit and visa steps. The candidate gathers documents, arranges insurance and decides whether family will join.

    What you do

    • Give the candidate a clear checklist and your start date.
    • Remind the candidate that Indian documents, such as degree certificates and police clearance, usually need an apostille and a sworn translation, which takes time.
    • Explain what you will cover, such as flights, temporary housing, a relocation allowance or a language course.
    • Ask about family. Family members can often join, and under the EU Blue Card a spouse can usually work.
    • Check social security. India has agreements with several Member States that can prevent double contributions, so ask the candidate to request a certificate of coverage where one applies.
    • India’s eMigrate portal and emigration clearance apply only to people taking up work in the 18 countries on the Emigration Check Required list. No EU Member State is on that list, so a candidate taking an EU job does not need to register on eMigrate. Rules can change, so ask the candidate to confirm on emigrate.gov.in or with the Ministry of External Affairs.

    Watch out for: health insurance must usually be in place from the first day.

    Step 6: Travel and arrival

    The candidate travels once the visa is issued and must enter within the visa's validity.

    What you do

    • Plan the start date with a margin, since permits and visas can be delayed.
    • Arrange first-night accommodation and, where you can, an airport pickup.
    • Give the candidate a short arrival checklist and a named contact in your company.

    Watch out for: the visa is not the final permit. In most countries the residence permit card is issued or collected after arrival.

    Step 7: Register and collect the residence permit

    Shortly after arrival, the candidate completes the local formalities. Deadlines are set by each country and are often short.

    What you do

    • Help the candidate book the registration and residence permit appointments.
    • Support the first practical tasks: registering an address, getting a tax number and social security number, opening a bank account and enrolling in health insurance.
    • Complete any employer confirmation your country requires once work begins.

    Watch out for: missing a registration deadline can cause problems with the permit, so put the dates in the candidate's arrival checklist.

    Step 8: Onboard and stay compliant

    Employment starts, and so do your ongoing obligations as the employer of a permit holder.

    What you do

    • Pay at least the salary on which the permit was granted, and keep the role in line with the permit.
    • Tell the authorities about changes, such as a new role, a changed salary or an end of employment, where your country requires it.
    • Note the permit's renewal date and start the renewal early.
    • Offer a structured onboarding, a mentor, workplace and cultural orientation, and language support. Include the family where you can.

    Watch out for: rules on changing employer or role differ by country and permit, so check before any change.

    The employee’s rights (EU Blue Card holders; other permits are similar, but check national rules)

    • Equal treatment with nationals on working conditions, pay, dismissal and health and safety at work, plus recognition of qualifications and access to education and social security, with some national limits.
    • Changing employer: in the first 12 months, a Member State may require notification or a labour market check. After that, usually only notification.
    • Losing a job does not by itself end the permit. Generally at least three months are allowed to find new work, but this varies by country.
    • Family: spouses and children can join under simplified family reunification, and spouses generally have access to work.
    • After 12 months, the holder can move to another Member State under simplified rules, and time worked in several Member States counts towards EU long-term residence.
    • The candidate should never pay recruitment fees, and the employer should not hold the candidate’s passport or documents.

    The employer’s rights and obligations

    • You may direct work within the contract and the permit, apply national probation and notice rules, and end the employment lawfully under national labour law.
    • You must pay at least the salary on which the permit was granted, give the same terms as comparable national employees, and keep the role in line with the permit.
    • Some countries add specific duties. Sweden requires health, life, occupational injury and occupational pension insurance for the whole permit period. The Netherlands requires recognised sponsors to keep proof that the salary was actually paid, and to notify the authority if it falls below the criterion.
    • Notify the authorities of changes, such as a new role, a changed salary or the end of employment, where your country requires it.

    Hire ethically and spot red flags

    The safest hires are made directly or through transparent channels, and the candidate never pays to get a job.

    • Do not charge candidates recruitment fees, and do not accept fees from agents charged to candidates.
    • Put the offer in writing before the candidate takes any step, and make sure the terms in the contract match the offer.
    • Be wary of intermediaries who promise visas, guarantee approvals or ask for payment to "hold" a job.
    • Use official government portals and authorities for every application.
