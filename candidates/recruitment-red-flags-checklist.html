Recruitment red flags checklist

The recruitment red flags specific to the India–EU corridor that a candidate may meet during the migration cycle, and how to verify and report them.

Recruitment fraud affects every kind of prospective migrant worker, from highly qualified ICT and healthcare professionals to seasonal and semi-skilled candidates.

Anchored in Objective 6 of the Global Compact for Migration and in international fair-recruitment standards, this checklist sets out the red flags specific to the India–EU corridor. Because generative AI now multiplies cross-border recruitment deception, it also covers new AI-enabled patterns, such as deepfake video interviews and AI-generated letters about qualifications, recognition or authorities.

1. Unverifiable recruiter and agency identity

  • No demonstrable legal status. Legal cross-border recruiters work under a licensing or registration regime. Verify the EU recruiter, company or agency through the Business Registers Interconnection System (BRIS) on the European e-Justice Portal. Its “Find a company” search returns company information directly from the national business registers of the EU and EEA countries.
  • No verifiable physical presence. A recruiter reachable only by a personal mobile number or a webmail address, with no registered office or company email domain matching the agency’s website, is a red flag. EURES recommends searching the recruiter’s name with the words “scam” or “fraud” to find earlier reports.
  • Impersonation of licensed employers or public officials. Documented cases use real logos and signatures. Check directly with the employer’s central human-resources office, using contact details you found yourself on the company website.

2. Ambiguous job description and content

  • Job description that is vague, generic or inconsistent with the employer’s stated business. A ‘software engineer’ role at a company with no technology footprint , an ‘IT’ post at a hospitality entity fails the coherence test. This is a characteristic feature of trafficking cases involving deceptive recruitment : the 2024-2025 trafficking of Indians into Southeast Asian cyber-scam (UNODC, October 2024) characteristically began with ‘IT’ , ‘Data-entry’ and ‘digital marketing’ offers advertised through social media.
  • Salary or benefit promises materially inconsistent with the Member State’s labour market. Under Directive (EU)2021/1883, the EU Blue Card salary threshold must sit between 1.0 and 1.6 times the destination Member State’s average gross national annual salary. A recruiter offering a Blue Card-eligible salary below the proposed threshold or far above the market wage should be checked against the EUROSTAT data and the destination’s published salary tables . For example, in the case of Germany, the annual thresholds are published on the Make it in Germany portal.
  • Guaranteed visa approvals, residence permit or citizenship. Visa and residence-permit decisions are the exclusive competence of Member State immigration authorities and cannot be guaranteed by any recruiter. Advertised guarantees are per se indicators of fraud.

3. Conspicuous communication, process and behavioural indicators

  • Approach through informal digital channels. Initial contact via WhatsApp, Instagram DM, Telegram, TikTok or Facebook Messenger, rather than corporate email, EURES portal message, employer careers site or LinkedIn Recruiter is the starting point of digital recruitment fraud identified in ICMPD’s The digital trap : How fake job ads exploit aspiring migrants and in the EURES editorial dated 15 June 2023. ICMPD documents the pattern of fake offer letters issued within hours followed by escalating fee requests.

    Further, a 2025 European Parliamentary Research Service briefing stated that ‘in the EU, scammers have impersonated Europol agents’ . For India-EU candidates, the medium is spoofed correspondence claiming to originate form a Member State Immigration authority, embassy or consulate or an AI-generated letter on synthetic letterhead confirming ‘visa pre-approval’, ‘priority processing’ or ‘employer sponsorship registration. The standard countermeasure in such cases is verification against the actual Member State authority through contact details sourced from official websites.

  • Absence of a structured selection process. Legitimate EU employers conduct structured selection processes which includes CV screening, at least one interview (usually video) , technical or professional assessment where applicable, reference checks and a formal offer on the company letterhead. Absence of any interview or an ‘interview’ limited to a typing test or a brief chat, is a documented pattern in the Ireland nursing fraud cases in 2024 and in the Southeast Asia cyber-scam trafficking.
  • Pressure tactics and manufactured urgency. Statements such as ‘start this week’ , ‘final approval today’, ‘we have only one seat left’ or ‘pay now to secure the visa slot’ are consistent with recruitment-fraud documented across European recruitment-industry analyses. Such manufactured urgency is inconsistent with the multi-week timelines of real EU work-permit processes.
  • Requests for sensitive personal data before any selection stage. Requests for passport bio-apge scans, biometric photographs, bank account details, salary certificates or income-tax returns before a documented formal application stage are not standard EU practice and expose candidates to ‘Identity theft’. EURES explicitly states ‘Never submit financial information via online forms during the recruitment process. No reputable company will start by asking for your bank or card details’.

4. Financial indicators

  • Any recruitment fee or related cost charged to the worker. Under the ILO General Principles and Operational Guidelines for Fair Recruitment (2016) and its definition of recruitment fees and related costs (2018), the employer pays for recruitment, not the worker. IRIS Principle 1, ILO Convention No. 181, Article 7 and Dhaka Principle 1 (the Employer Pays Principle) say the same. For EU single-permit routes, Article 10 of Directive (EU) 2024/1233 adds that the employer pays the permit fees and may not recover them from the worker.
  • Fees under other names. “Processing”, “training”, “orientation”, “visa deposit”, “security deposit”, a language course tied to the offer, “medical examination”, “police clearance”, “seat reservation” or “priority visa” fees all fall within the ILO definition of recruitment fees, and may not be charged to the worker.
  • Payment through informal or untraceable channels. A request to pay into a personal bank account, in cash, by money transfer to an individual, with gift cards or through a company registered outside the destination country is a red flag. Make it in Germany is plain: German law prohibits charging applicants for placement or advice, and anyone asking you for money for a job or visa support is not legitimate. Most other Member States have equivalent rules.
  • Loans, advances or credit from the recruiter or a partner. An offer to finance your fees against future work is a warning of debt bondage, and goes against fair recruitment.

The Employer Pays Principle

IHRB Dhaka Principles (2012), IOM IRIS Standard, ILO Fair Recruitment (2016, 2018)

What the worker may pay

  • Nothing for recruitment services.
  • No processing, training, orientation, visa deposit, security deposit, medical or police-clearance fees.
  • No salary deductions to repay any of the above.

What the employer must pay

  • All costs of recruitment services.
  • Visa and single-permit processing fees (Directive (EU) 2024/1233, Article 10).
  • Transport, medical and administrative costs needed to take up the post.

Dhaka Principles: ihrb.org · IOM IRIS Standard: iris.iom.int · ILO Fair Recruitment: ilo.org

5. Contract and documentation indicators

  • Absence of a written contract before departure. International standards require migrant workers to receive a written contract, in a language they understand, before departure. Any arrangement whereby ‘the contract will be signed on arrival’ is a documented pattern in contract-substitution fraud.
  • Divergence between verbal promises and written contract terms. Wages, benefits, working hours, leave, contract duration, notice period, accommodation, cost of return transport and termination clauses that differ from what was verbally promised or that differ between the version signed in India and the version presented on arrival in the Member State constitute contract substitution.
  • Refusal to provide a copy of the contract for retention. The candidate’s retention of an original signed contract before departure is standard practice. Refusal, including on the grounds that ‘the original stays with the employer’ or ‘you will get it after arrival’ is an indicator of exploitative intent.
  • One-sided ancillary clauses. Clauses imposing disproportionate penalties for early termination by the worker, clauses tying accommodation and transport exclusively to the employer with rent deductions from wages, clauses requiring the worker to surrender identity documents to the employer and mandatory arbitration clauses in the employer’s home jurisdiction that effectively deny the worker access to Member State labour courts deviate from the equal-treatment standard of Directive (EU) 2024/1233

6. Visa route, housing and identification documents

  • Retention of passport or identity-document by the recruiter or employer. Even if framed as ‘safekeeping’, ‘for visa processing’ or ‘until fees is paid’, such retention by a recruiter or employer is prohibited by IRIS Principle 2 and Dhaka Principle 4.
  • Instruction or advise to travel on a visa that does not authorize employment. Entry to an EU Member State on a short-stay Schengen visa (Type C, up to 90 days in 180) or a tourist visa to ‘start work’ and ‘regularize later’ is unlawful and makes the candidate susceptible to removal under national law. Legal EU labour mobility uses the national work visa/single permit (Directive (EU)2024/1233), the Blue Card, the Intra-Corporate Transferee permit (Directive 2014/66/EU), the Researcher/Student permit or a national scheme.
  • Instruction to enter on a student, tourist or family visa with intent to convert to work. Except for the specific in-country change of status pathways permitted by the destination Member State’s national law, induced misuse of a non-work visa is fraud on the immigration authority.
  • Requirement to surrender biometric enrolment or immigration correspondence to the recruiter. Legitimate immigration procedures require the applicant’s personal appearance at biometric enrolment for eg. At the Visa Application Centre or Consulate. A recruiter who is claiming to ‘handle biometrics on your behalf’ or intercepts official correspondence with the Member State authority is committing fraud.

7. Digital and online indicators

Generative AI has brought a marked rise in AI-enabled recruitment fraud. It is now used at every stage of the migration cycle, through deepfake interviews, cloned voices and fake letters from authorities.

  • Look-alike domains. Domains that copy a real one with a changed letter or an added hyphen, or with “.org”, “.info” or “.com” in place of the real ending, are a common form of employer impersonation. Check the sender’s domain against the company website you found through your own search, never through a link the recruiter sent.
  • Job ads on aggregator sites that are not on the employer’s careers page. Confirm the vacancy with the employer’s human-resources contact on the official company domain before you engage.
  • AI-generated job ads and fake company profiles. ICMPD’s The digital trap (2025) and industry sources record more AI-generated offer letters, fake company websites and synthetic recruiter profiles on LinkedIn. Signs include fewer than 50 connections, no post history, stock profile photos and an inconsistent work history.

Even with these checks, deepfake video interviews make generative AI a powerful tool for recruitment fraud.

Deepfake video interview: detection markers

Adapted from FBI IC3 Public Service Announcement I-062822-PY (28 June 2022)

Audio and video do not match

  • Lip movements do not fully match the words spoken.
  • Coughs, sneezes or other sounds do not match what is shown.

Reused identity data

  • Stolen personal data used to pass background checks.
  • Fake identities built from breached data sold on the dark web.

Behaviour

  • Reluctance to change the camera angle or move closer to it.
  • Refusal to join an unscheduled follow-up video call at short notice.

Image

  • Frame-rate glitches at the edges of the face, especially hair and jawline.
  • Uniform, over-smooth skin; lighting on the face that does not match.

Reference: FBI IC3, PSA I-062822-PY, ic3.gov/PSA/2022/psa220628

Mitigation measures

  • Verify the interviewer as well as the offer: check their identity against the employer’s official directory or company LinkedIn page, using contact details you found yourself.
  • Insist on at least one live, in-person or supervised identity check before you sign or pay anything. On EU work-permit routes this happens anyway at biometric enrolment at the visa application centre: use it to confirm who the recruiter is.
  • Verify every “recognition” letter directly with the destination country’s ENIC-NARIC centre, through the ENIC-NARIC portal.
  • Treat voice notes, calls and short video messages as unverified until they are confirmed on another channel.
  • Report suspected AI-enabled recruitment fraud. In India, report it to the Indian mission in the destination country and on the MADAD portal. In the EU, report it to the country’s national cybercrime unit, and to the national anti-trafficking coordinator if there are signs of trafficking.
